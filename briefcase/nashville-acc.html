Introduction

I am Jade Zhao, an Informatics student at Indiana University Bloomington in the Luddy School of Informatics, Computing, and Engineering. I am a first-generation student. My minors are Business Technology and user experience (UX) research. I am interested in how a public system holds up once a real person has to finish a task on it.

On this project my technical work is reading page structure, mapping checks to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), and keeping a small team's tasks visible. The work I want sits in that same place: accessibility, client communication, and software that a community partner can actually use.

Client

The client is the Town of Nashville, Indiana. The site under assessment is townofnashville.org. Our scope names Maddie Bond, Town of Nashville Administrative Assistant, as the client contact. I coordinate with Maya before client outreach.

The town site is the public information hub for administration, water and sewer utilities, the Clerk-Treasurer, Town Council records, police, boards and commissions, development review, parks and arts, and links out to Brown County services. People use it to pay a bill, start or stop water service, find a meeting, read an ordinance, or act on a time-sensitive notice.

Our stakeholder research, drawn from the scope of work and from public pages on the town site in early September 2026, treats people with disabilities as part of every audience. They need the same civic tasks to work with a screen reader, magnification, a keyboard, captions, or plain language. One check in the team folder also records a client ask: make contact, parking, and disability-access help easier to find for residents with hearing or visual impairments. A formal town-wide mission statement was not on the pages we reviewed. Commission pages, such as the Town Park Commission and the Arts and Entertainment Commission, publish their own purpose statements.

Process and solution

We organised the semester as assessment work. Setup, accessibility training, stakeholder research, then structured checks. Findings go into a shared report for the town and for the ServeIT web team. We do not change the live site.

Before the client conversation I prepared a one-page agenda: introductions, what access problems the town has already noticed, a short mapping of users and needs, how we would check against WCAG, and time to set a later presentation. The September agenda still left the final presentation date open. I am not treating that presentation as delivered.

After that, the team split the checks. Local final-report files cover carousels and rotating banners, contact / parking / disability-access information, headings and content structure, page titles, font size and colour contrast, and keyboard-only use. Each write-up records the page, what was tested, a rating, the relevant WCAG criterion, and a recommended correction. The solution at this point in the semester is that recommendation set, ready for handoff. The town decides what to change.

Reflection

The lesson I wrote down from the Deque training panel, How Persons with Disabilities Use the Web, is the one I am using on this team. Fatigue came from small barriers stacking up: weak headings, clutter, inconsistent navigation, and tasks that assume everyone has the same energy left in the day. When I write a finding, I want the note to say what that extra effort costs, and to be specific enough that someone else can recommend a correction.

The same notes changed how wide I set the check. Keyboard use, structure, and plain language sit beside colour contrast. The report should help the town improve the site residents already use. On the lead side, a Planner card with a name and an honest Done status is how I can see the week. Praise can sit in the team channel. Corrective notes stay private and about the work.
