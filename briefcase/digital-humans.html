AI research · not a launch

01 / Research question

Digital Humans

How can digital humans make AI interactions more useful, understandable, and trustworthy?

Public, high-level case study ... not peer-reviewed lab claims, not a product page.

02 / Context

Why this problem matters

I am an Informatics student at Luddy and the student project lead for Digital Humans. Digital Humans are not just avatars. They are interfaces that borrow the social cues of a person: face, voice, timing, a sense that someone is there. That can make a system easier to approach and easier to misunderstand.

Industry pages sell 24/7 service and a persona that never gets tired. Policy writing asks for transparency, risk assessment, and a human when the system should stop. My work sits in that gap. Adoption can rise when the interface feels human. Trust only holds if people can see that it is a machine, correct what it inferred, and reach a person.

The product question is not whether AI can seem human. It is whether AI can help people show up more honestly without replacing the human relationship.

Working rule: coach the person, protect the connection, and never let a persuasive interface hide what it is doing.

03 / My role

What I actually do

Where this sits

This is student research at Luddy, not a classroom product. I am not claiming a Digital Human was deployed for teaching, grading, or advising. The education question I keep is the same as the business one: if a system talks like a person, can a student (or a guest) see what it is, pause it, and reach a human?

05 / Research process

How I work, without inventing a study

I do not have a published tool-evaluation table. I have not run a user study I can cite here. What I can show is the process I use before anyone talks about shipping.

  • Write the question in plain language before naming a platform.
  • Read public industry explainers that treat Digital Humans as speech, vision, and language stacked into a persona, and public risk-management writing (including the NIST AI Risk Management Framework) for map, measure, and manage vocabulary.
  • Separate embodied cues from a text chatbot. A face and a voice add social presence. They also add pressure to trust the system as if it were a person.
  • Ask what the system can infer, what people can see, what they can consent to, and who is accountable when it fails.

Those readings are context. They are not my experiments and not a claim that I implemented a vendor stack.

06 / Criteria

Design tests I will stand behind

In HCI (human-computer interaction), disclosure cannot be a footnote. It has to match what the system actually does, at the moment a person needs to decide.

  • Can a person inspect and correct the profile being used?
  • Can they delete it or refuse at each stage?
  • Is the system disclosed before the first answer, not after someone has already confided?
  • Does the system reduce friction without hiding what it is?
  • Is there a clean path back to a human when the system should stop talking?

Design test, not a product

One proxy I use: a system that promises to cut the unpaid labour of browsing people. The risk is that it turns insecurities into data, preferences into rankings, and a recommendation into a decision that feels inevitable. The test is the same as above. Disclose what was inferred, show why a recommendation appeared, and let the person correct, refuse, or leave.

That is a thought example for recommendation interfaces. It is not a dating product on this site, and it is not a user-count claim.

07 / Interaction flow

A path I would test, not a shipped demo

No prototype screenshots are in this folder. This is the sequence I would walk with any interface that looks like a person. It is a research sketch.

  1. Arrive. The page says it is a machine before the first personal question.
  2. Ask. The person can type. Voice is optional, never required.
  3. Pause. They can stop the turn and read what the system thinks it knows.
  4. Correct or refuse. Edit the profile, delete it, or leave.
  5. Handoff. A clean path to a human when the system should stop talking.

08 / Technical exploration

What I look at, and what I have not shipped

A Digital Human is usually sold as a stack: speech in, a language model, a face or voice out. I care less about whether that stack can be assembled and more about where it fails a person.

  • Anthropomorphism: once a system looks at you, people may grant it more competence than the logs support.
  • Disclosure timing: a label in a footer is too late if the first turn already asked for something personal.
  • Human oversight: someone has to be able to interrupt. Voice can be an option. It must never be a requirement. Keyboard and switch access have to work. Motion needs a reduced-motion path.
  • Retrieval versus fluency: a fluent answer is not evidence that the system found the right note. That is why I also keep a small, inspectable BM25 notes search ... keyword ranking you can read line by line, without pretending it understands.

Prototype boundary I will not cross in public work: do not fabricate a person's identity, do not replace a human partner, and do not send automated messages to a real person without their knowledge.

09 / Working conclusions

What I will say, and what I will not

These are conclusions I use to judge work. They are not results from a published study.

  • A human-like interface can help people start. It can also hide the machine at the exact moment they need to decide.
  • The business case is not “make it human.” The case is whether people can trust it enough to keep using it, and whether a human can take over.
  • A demo is not a finished system. A warm voice is not evidence of understanding.

11 / Reflection

What the research taught me

I grew up in a family restaurant. A system either survives contact with a real person or it does not. Clinic handoffs taught the same thing: after the demo, someone without a developer on call has to keep the page. Digital Humans is that instinct aimed at interfaces that talk back.

If a sentence sounds like a launch, it does not belong here. The next honest artifact is either a shareable prototype still with these tests, or a note that says why it failed them.
